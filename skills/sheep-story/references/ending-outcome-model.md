# Ending Outcome Model

## Purpose

Use this reference when planning, classifying, comparing, or reviewing story endings where `happy`, `bad`, `bittersweet`, `tragic`, or `pyrrhic` is too coarse.

The core rule is:

> An ending is not one scalar mood. Different hopes can succeed or fail at the same time, costs can belong to different time periods, and recovery can be partial even when the governing mission succeeds.

This reference complements `reader-promise-and-tone.md`. Reader Promise defines the emotional contract; this model defines what actually survives, fails, costs, heals, or remains possible at the end.

Do not force every story into a tragedy template. Pressure, danger, sacrifice, and irreversible consequence are tools. The ending may be restorative, hopeful, bittersweet, tragic, comic, open, cyclical, transformative, or deliberately low-friction if that matches the project contract.

## Core Model

Prefer:

```text
Ending
=
Outcome Vector
+ Story-Incurred Cost
+ Recovery / Restoration
+ Future Possibility / Meaning
```

Use the four-quadrant `Hope × Cost` model only as a compact summary after the underlying dimensions are clear.

## 1. Outcome Vector: Whose Hope Succeeds?

Do not ask only:

> Did the story end happily?

Ask which outcome domain is being measured.

### Common Outcome Domains

```markdown
Personal survival / autonomy:
Personal governing desire:
Romantic / relationship outcome:
Family / friendship outcome:
Mission / tactical objective:
Community / institutional outcome:
World / civilization outcome:
Moral / thematic value preserved:
Future possibility:
```

A story can simultaneously produce:

```text
world saved                 = success
mission completed           = success
romance                      = failure
protagonist survives        = success
protagonist fully recovers  = failure
future worth living         = success
```

This is not contradictory. It is a mixed outcome vector.

### Scope Before Label

Before calling an ending `happy`, `tragic`, or `low-cost`, state the scope:

```markdown
Primary ending subject:
Secondary ending subjects:
Whose desire defines the summary label:
Which outcomes are deliberately not included in that label:
```

A peace treaty may be a world-level success while two central characters do not become a couple. A protagonist may die while the civilization they protected survives. A romance may succeed while the political mission fails.

Do not average these too early.

## 2. Cost: What Was Actually Lost?

Cost should track irreversible or difficult-to-reverse loss, not merely danger, screen time, or how grim the setting looks.

Possible cost dimensions:

- death
- permanent injury or disability
- psychological trauma
- relationship rupture
- exile / displacement
- loss of home
- loss of status / authority
- loss of wealth / equipment / institution
- moral injury
- reputation
- cultural loss
- extinction / population loss
- time / youth / missed life
- irreversible transformation
- promises that can no longer be fulfilled

### Danger Is Not Cost

```text
almost died twenty times
+ everyone returns intact
```

may still be a relatively low-cost ending.

Conversely:

```text
quiet final chapter
+ one permanent relationship loss
```

may carry high personal cost.

### Inherited Cost vs Story-Incurred Cost

Keep these separate.

#### Inherited / Background Cost

Damage that existed before the story's main action or before the protagonist could affect it:

- civilization already collapsed
- planet already devastated
- species already near extinction
- old war already destroyed the homeland
- protagonist already lost a family member before chapter one

This shapes the Reader Promise and environment but should not automatically inflate the ending's story-incurred cost.

#### Story-Incurred Cost

Loss caused, suffered, or made irreversible during the story being evaluated.

#### Terminal Cost

Loss specifically paid in the final movement or as the price of resolution.

Use:

```markdown
Inherited / background cost:
Story-incurred cost:
Terminal / resolution cost:
Who paid each cost:
Which losses remain irreversible:
```

### Cost Distribution

Do not assume the protagonist personally pays every cost.

Track:

```text
protagonist cost
party / fellowship cost
civilian cost
institutional cost
world cost
antagonist cost
future-generation cost
```

A "happy ending for the hero" can still sit on a catastrophic civilian cost. A heroic sacrifice can create high personal cost but high world hope.

## 3. Recovery / Restoration

Recovery is independent from whether the goal succeeded.

Track relevant domains:

```markdown
Physical recovery:
Psychological recovery:
Relationship repair:
Home / community restoration:
Institutional recovery:
Ecological / world recovery:
Memory / identity recovery:
Economic / material recovery:
Time horizon:
```

### Reset Is Not Required

A hopeful ending does not require returning to the pre-story state.

Distinguish:

#### Restorative Ending

Much of what was damaged can be repaired and ordinary life resumes or improves.

#### Transformative Ending

The old world cannot return, but a new viable life becomes possible.

#### Partial Recovery

The mission succeeds, but some wounds remain permanent.

#### No Recovery

The damage remains dominant or the surviving characters cannot meaningfully rebuild.

### Recovery Can Be Uneven

```text
world recovery        = high
relationship recovery = low
physical recovery     = low
future possibility    = high
```

This often produces a bittersweet or earned-happy ending rather than a clean restoration.

## 4. Hope / Future Possibility

Hope does not mean `nothing bad happened`.

Operationally, hope asks:

> After the ending's losses are acknowledged, does the story still establish a future in which the governing value, relationship, community, or way of life can meaningfully continue?

Hope can survive permanent loss.

Useful forms:

- someone survives to continue the work
- a community remains
- a war ends
- a new institution becomes possible
- a relationship changes but remains meaningful
- a sacrifice prevents annihilation
- the next generation receives a better option
- mercy or trust creates a later solution
- the protagonist learns how to live with an irreversible wound

### Hope Is Not Optimistic Dialogue

A character saying `everything will be okay` does not create a hopeful ending if the story's causal system shows that agency, repair, and future choice are impossible.

Likewise, a grim final image does not make the ending hopeless if the story has preserved meaningful future possibility.

## Hope × Cost Quadrant

After scope is defined, the compact two-axis model can be useful.

| | Lower Story-Incurred Cost | Higher Story-Incurred Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Hope / governing value succeeds | Restorative / clean happy / low-friction victory | Earned happy / bittersweet hopeful / sacrificial victory / eucatastrophic turn |
| Hope / governing value fails | Wistful loss / graceful failure / unfulfilled desire | Tragedy / catastrophic defeat / downfall |

Do not use quadrant position as the final classification. Recovery and outcome scope often distinguish stories inside the same quadrant.

## Ending Modes

### Clean / Restorative Happy Ending

```text
primary hope succeeds
+ story-incurred cost low or recoverable
+ recovery high
+ future stable
```

The story may contain intense danger without demanding permanent loss.

### Earned Happy Ending

```text
hope succeeds
+ meaningful cost
+ character choices materially caused the success
+ recovery substantial
```

The happiness feels earned because the story made failure believable and choices consequential.

### Bittersweet Hopeful Ending

```text
important hope succeeds
+ high irreversible cost
+ recovery partial
+ future remains worth entering
```

The world does not reset. The ending preserves meaning without pretending the damage vanished.

### Sacrificial Victory

```text
mission / world hope succeeds
+ one or more central characters pay severe or terminal personal cost
```

Do not label this automatically tragic. The outcome vector may be strongly hopeful at community or world scope while personally devastating.

### Eucatastrophic Turn

A credible disaster becomes a sudden upward turn that fulfills earlier causal preparation, mercy, loyalty, courage, or providential structure.

Use only when:

- disaster was genuinely plausible
- the turn does not erase prior agency
- earlier choices or story rules make the turn retrospectively coherent
- the turn preserves rather than trivializes cost

Do not use `miracle` as a substitute for setup.

### Wistful / Graceful Loss

```text
central desire fails
+ cost remains bounded
+ character can continue living
+ experience retains meaning
```

Common in romance, coming-of-age, and stories about accepting impermanence.

### Pyrrhic Victory

Use narrowly.

A Pyrrhic victory is not simply `success + high cost`.

It requires that the cost seriously undermines, nearly cancels, or calls into question the value of the victory itself.

Ask:

```markdown
What was won?
What was lost to win it?
Does the loss destroy much of what made the objective valuable?
Would the characters make the same trade again knowing the price?
```

If the world is saved at terrible personal cost but the saved future clearly remains valuable, `sacrificial` or `bittersweet hopeful` may be more precise than `pyrrhic`.

### Tragedy

A high-cost ending where the governing hope or value fails, often through choices, limitations, misunderstandings, institutions, or flaws that remain causally intelligible.

Tragedy is not synonymous with body count.

### Catastrophic Defeat

The mission or world-level goal fails and large irreversible damage occurs.

This can be an intermediate ending in a multi-part story rather than the final thematic judgment.

### Transformative Ending

The original desired state cannot return, but the characters establish a different viable future.

Transformative can combine with happy, bittersweet, or tragic elements.

### Open / Ambiguous Ending

The story deliberately leaves one or more outcome dimensions unresolved while still answering enough of the governing emotional question to feel intentional.

Do not use ambiguity to avoid making a decision the story promised to answer.

## Personal Ending vs World Ending

Always separate these when they diverge.

Example shapes:

```text
Personal: survives but loses beloved
World: saved
Relationship: permanently lost
Recovery: partial
→ high-cost hopeful / sacrificial-bittersweet
```

```text
Personal: dies
World: saved
Value: carried forward by others
→ sacrificial victory
```

```text
Personal: safe and fulfilled
World: devastated
→ locally happy, globally catastrophic
```

This prevents protagonist-centric classification from erasing the rest of the story.

## Relationship Outcome Is Its Own Axis

Do not treat `they did not become a couple` as proof of a bad ending.

Track:

```markdown
Romantic desire fulfilled?:
Mutual care preserved?:
Relationship transformed?:
Separation voluntary / imposed?:
Does either character retain a viable future?:
Did the relationship accomplish its thematic function?:
```

A romance can fail while the characters, mission, and world succeed.

Likewise, a couple can get together inside an otherwise catastrophic ending.

## Hope and Recovery Engine

Bad outcomes have causal engines; good outcomes need them too.

Avoid:

```text
suffering
→ suffering
→ suffering
→ author declares a happy ending
```

Prefer:

```text
pressure
→ reason to continue
→ costly choice
→ choice creates trust / information / alliance / mercy / competence
→ earlier prosocial action returns as a real option
→ final problem becomes solvable
→ success leaves residual cost
→ recovery converts survival into a future
```

### Persistence Must Buy Something Causal

When characters keep going, track what persistence produces:

- more information
- time for rescue or coordination
- trust
- fellowship
- a witness
- a repaired relationship
- mastery
- political legitimacy
- an ally's reciprocal action
- accumulated small advantages
- preservation of a moral line
- survival until circumstances change

Persistence should not be rewarded merely because the protagonist is the protagonist.

### Fellowship Formation

A group becoming a true fellowship can itself be an outcome engine:

```text
separate goals
+ mistrust
→ forced cooperation
→ failure / shared cost
→ mutual recognition
→ chosen loyalty
→ distributed capability
→ final options no individual possessed alone
```

The team existing on paper is not the same as fellowship.

### Mercy / Kindness Payoff

A prior act of mercy, trust, restraint, or kindness can create a later solution if the causal chain remains visible.

This is especially useful for eucatastrophic turns because the upward reversal feels both surprising and earned.

## Courage Is Independent of Ending Success

A character may choose courage without receiving a happy ending.

Keep separate:

```text
fear response
choice under fear
commitment persistence
actual outcome
```

The moral or thematic value of continuing can survive tactical defeat.

Conversely, a successful ending does not prove the protagonist acted courageously.

## Multi-Part Outcome Movement

A franchise, season, or multi-volume story can deliberately move between quadrants.

Example:

```text
Part 1 ending:
high cost + governing hope fails
→ catastrophic defeat

Part 2:
characters inherit the real cost rather than resetting
→ recovery attempt
→ new choices
→ hope succeeds at additional cost
```

The second victory becomes heavier because the first defeat remains causal state.

Do not erase a previous ending's cost merely because the sequel wants a new adventure.

## Ending Scope Errors

Flag these common mistakes.

### Romance-Centric Error

`The couple did not end together, therefore bad ending.`

Fix: classify relationship outcome separately from personal, mission, and world outcomes.

### Protagonist-Centric Error

`The protagonist survived, therefore happy ending.`

Fix: inspect civilian, community, institutional, and world cost.

### Background-Catastrophe Inflation

`The setting says humanity previously lost 99%, therefore this story has an ultra-high-cost ending.`

Fix: separate inherited background cost from cost incurred during the evaluated story.

### Body-Count Tragedy Error

`Many characters died, therefore tragedy.`

Fix: ask whether the governing value or hope ultimately failed.

### Reset Happy Ending

A finale declares happiness while injuries, grief, destroyed institutions, or broken relationships vanish without causal recovery.

### Mandatory Suffering

The story punishes characters only because the author believes a serious ending must hurt.

Respect the Reader Promise. Low-cost or overwhelmingly successful endings are valid when intentionally promised and dramatically supported.

### Fake Pyrrhic Label

Calling every expensive victory `pyrrhic` even though the result clearly remains worth the cost.

### Recovery Equals Cure

Assuming a character cannot have a hopeful future unless every injury, grief, disability, or trauma is removed.

Recovery may mean adaptation, support, meaning, new routine, changed relationships, or a different life.

## Review Questions

Before approving a finale ask:

1. Which hope is being called successful or failed?
2. Are relationship, personal, mission, and world outcomes being conflated?
3. Which costs existed before the story and which were caused during it?
4. Who actually paid the cost?
5. What is permanently lost?
6. What can recover, and by what mechanism?
7. Does the future remain meaningful?
8. Did good outcomes arise from prior choices, relationships, information, mercy, persistence, or established world rules?
9. Does a sudden positive turn preserve the credibility of prior danger?
10. Does the ending honor the Reader Promise without requiring mechanical suffering or mechanical happiness?

## Principle

A happy ending is not a world without scars. A tragedy is not a story with many corpses.

Classify what succeeded, what was paid, what can heal, and what future remains possible. Then let the ending's emotional label emerge from those facts.
