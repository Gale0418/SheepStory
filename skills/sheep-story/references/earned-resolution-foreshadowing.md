# Earned Resolution and Foreshadowing

## Purpose

Use this reference when planning or reviewing clues, foreshadowing, reveals, reversals, climax solutions, character mistakes, surprise rescues, sudden defeats, or endings where the reader may ask:

> Did the story earn this, or did the author simply move a piece by hand?

The core rule is:

> **Complications and resolutions should reveal or reward established causality.**

A surprising event may be fair. A character may make a terrible mistake. An outside force may intervene. A long-range weapon may decide a battle. The question is not whether the event is unusual; the question is whether the story established enough cause, agency, capability, motivation, and consequence for the event to feel like part of the world rather than a patch applied by the author.

This reference complements:

- `story-architecture.md` for large-scale causal shape
- `continuity/promises/` for setup/payoff lifecycle
- `ending-outcome-model.md` for final outcome, cost, recovery, and future possibility
- `character-conditions-advantage.md` for capability, resources, environment, and luck
- `character-memory-belief.md` for who knows what
- `genius-strategy.md` for high-level tactical reasoning

Do not load it for every ordinary scene. Use it when surprise, setup/payoff, competence, or resolution causality materially matters.

## 1. Stealth Foreshadowing Is Allowed — With an Approved Truth Boundary

SheepStory may plant subtle foreshadowing without announcing every clue inside the prose.

However, distinguish three things:

```text
underlying canon / reveal
!= textual clue placement
!= reader interpretation
```

### Safe Stealth Foreshadowing

Use when:

- the underlying truth, reveal, rule, object, identity, or payoff is already approved canon; or
- the user has explicitly delegated a bounded class of twist/reveal decisions and the current workflow permits that delegation; and
- the clue does not create an unapproved major plot fact outside that boundary.

Then SheepStory may quietly choose where to place clues, just as it may choose local gestures or sensory detail, provided the clue remains consistent with approved canon.

### Not Allowed as Silent Canon

Do not secretly decide, for example:

- who the murderer really is
- that an ally is a traitor
- that a protagonist has a hidden sibling
- that the world is a simulation
- that a dead character is secretly alive
- that a new superweapon exists

when those would be major canon-affecting decisions the user has not approved or delegated.

A model cannot turn `surprise for the reader` into `surprise canon imposed on the author`.

### Author-Secret vs Character-Secret

The author/user may know a reveal while characters and first-time readers do not. This is the normal state for controlled foreshadowing.

When the user wants to experience the story more like a reader, use `authoring-laboratory.md` for sandboxed alternatives or explicitly delegated twist generation. Do not silently promote a laboratory surprise into canon.

## 2. The Foreshadowing Contract

A useful clue has two lives:

```text
first read: ordinary / ambiguous / incomplete
second read: newly meaningful but still truthful
```

The ideal response is:

> "I did not notice that." → "But it was there." → "Now it makes sense."

Avoid:

> "There was no way to know that." → "The author just added it later."

### Foreshadowing Depth

Use the lightest depth that fits the promise.

#### Explicit Setup

The story openly establishes a tool, rule, threat, promise, or limitation that is expected to return.

Examples:

- a weapon is demonstrated early
- a door can only be opened from one side
- an ally promises to return
- a ritual has a stated cost

#### Camouflaged Setup

The information is visible but embedded in another purpose.

Examples:

- a family photo establishes a person who later matters
- a maintenance complaint establishes a later mechanical limit
- a joke demonstrates a rule
- a side argument reveals a relationship fault line

#### Latent / Reinterpretable Clue

The detail has an innocent first interpretation and a stronger second interpretation after the reveal.

Examples:

- a character reacts half a beat late to a familiar name
- an object is consistently missing from one location
- a witness uses unusually precise wording
- a person never answers one category of question directly

#### Negative-Space Clue

Absence itself becomes evidence only when the audience has enough baseline to notice it fairly.

Examples:

- a security system records every entrance except one known blind spot
- a meticulous character never mentions one expected person
- all survivors describe the sound except the supposed eyewitness

Do not use negative space when the reader was never given a reason to expect the missing information.

## 3. Foreshadowing Placement Techniques

Useful stealth methods include:

- **ordinary object state** — an object is introduced for a mundane reason before becoming important
- **rule demonstration** — show a small harmless case of a rule before the rule becomes decisive
- **minor-version rehearsal** — a low-stakes version anticipates a later high-stakes mechanism
- **asymmetric reaction** — one character reacts differently for a reason the reader cannot yet identify
- **misinterpretable statement** — literally true wording points toward the wrong inference without becoming a lie by the narration
- **background consequence** — a news item, repair bill, bureaucratic rule, or rumor establishes a mechanism indirectly
- **relationship habit** — repeated behavior creates the condition for later trust, betrayal, rescue, or misunderstanding
- **competence demonstration** — show a skill before the climax requires it
- **limitation demonstration** — show a weakness before the climax exploits it
- **motif with causal payload** — a recurring image or object matters mechanically or emotionally, not only symbolically

Do not highlight every clue with suspicious prose. If every important noun receives a spotlight, the text becomes a puzzle UI rather than a story.

## 4. Promise Weight and Reader Attention

Not every detail is a promise.

Reader expectation rises with:

```text
narrative emphasis
+ repetition
+ specificity
+ mystery framing
+ emotional weight
+ unusual placement
+ explicit unanswered question
```

A brightly emphasized locked box creates a larger payoff obligation than a cup casually mentioned on a table.

Use the existing promise lifecycle for story-bearing setups:

```text
proposed
→ open
→ advanced / reframed
→ paid / delayed / contradicted / intentionally-unresolved / retired
```

Do not create a promise file for every background detail. Track only details whose later absence would reasonably feel like a missing payoff.

## 5. Retrospective Fairness Test

After a reveal or major payoff, mentally reread the earlier scenes.

Ask:

1. Was the decisive mechanism already possible under established rules?
2. Did any earlier scene become literally false rather than merely reinterpreted?
3. Were relevant clues available before the answer?
4. If clues were hidden, were they hidden by natural attention rather than by the narration lying?
5. Did the reveal depend on information the viewpoint character would have noticed but the narration arbitrarily concealed?
6. Could a careful reader form at least the right class of hypothesis, even if not the exact answer?
7. Does the payoff give old details new meaning instead of invalidating them?

A reveal can remain difficult or nearly impossible to predict while still being retrospectively fair.

## 6. Setup Is Not the Same as Satisfaction

An element can be fully established and still produce a weak climax.

Distinguish:

```text
Existence Fairness: was the tool / force / rule established?
Causal Fairness: did it operate according to established rules?
Agency Fairness: did important characters materially shape the outcome?
Competence Fairness: did characters retain their established ability and judgment?
Catharsis Fit: did the resolution pay the kind of dramatic contest the story trained the audience to care about?
Reader-Promise Fit: did the resolution honor the promised experience?
```

This distinction matters for system-level weapons, legal rulings, political interventions, remote artillery, divine rules, diseases, or institutional power.

A long-range strike can be perfectly foreshadowed and tactically sensible yet still feel unsatisfying if the story spent most of its climax promise teaching the audience to expect a reciprocal close-range duel where character skill would decide the result.

Established does not automatically mean satisfying.

## 7. Crisis Causality: How Characters May Enter Trouble

A protagonist may be pushed into crisis by:

### Opponent Competence

The opponent sees a weakness, creates pressure, denies resources, or makes a strong move.

### Character-Consistent Mistake

A mistake grows from established:

- values
- fear
- attachment
- ambition
- pride
- impatience
- incomplete evidence
- overconfidence
- duty
- trauma response
- cognitive or social blind spot

The mistake should still look like something this person would plausibly do under this pressure.

### Opponent-Exploited Weakness

The strongest form often is:

```text
character has a meaningful tendency
→ opponent notices or predicts it
→ opponent creates a situation that activates it
→ character makes a locally plausible choice
→ crisis
```

This raises the opponent's stock without turning the victim into an idiot.

### Constraint Failure

Equipment, law, terrain, institutional access, fuel, ammunition, time, injury, politics, or communication limits may close options if established.

### Chance Complication

Coincidence may create trouble more freely than it resolves trouble because the character still has to answer the new problem.

Use chance as a question generator, not a substitute for the answer.

## 8. Cheap Crisis Patterns To Reject

### Ally Idiot Lever

```text
competent plan
→ ally forgets basic procedure for no character reason
→ disaster
```

This does not strengthen the opponent and often only damages investment in the ally.

### Competence Collapse

A character previously demonstrated as capable suddenly loses obvious strategic, social, technical, or political reasoning solely because the plot needs them to lose.

Before accepting an apparently foolish decision, ask:

```markdown
What information does the character have?
What alternatives would they normally consider?
What pressure or value closes those alternatives?
What changed since their earlier competent behavior?
Is the decision still characteristic rather than convenient?
```

Desperation can narrow strategy. Obsession can collapse optionality. A symbolic fixation can become a fatal blind spot. But the causal transition must exist on the page.

### Repeated Friendly Fire From Plot Convenience

One meaningful mistake can define a tragedy. Repeated unmotivated mistakes from the same side often become difficulty sliders controlled by the author.

### Arbitrary Resource Failure

Do not make the radio, gun, engine, key, or door fail only when the plot needs it unless the failure mode was established, probable, caused, or itself becomes a meaningful new problem.

## 9. Resolution Causality: What Should Earn Success

Strong resolutions typically credit one or more of:

- protagonist decision
- established skill
- learned adaptation
- accumulated information
- relationship trust
- fellowship / distributed capability
- sacrifice
- prior mercy or kindness
- use of an established resource
- exploitation of an established opponent blind spot
- induced opponent error
- endurance that bought time or information
- an established rule used in a new way

The resolution need not be a solo achievement. Shared victories are legitimate when the causal chain shows how the group became capable of winning together.

## 10. Induced Error Is Better Than Convenient Error

An enemy may make a mistake without losing credibility if the protagonist helped create it.

Prefer:

```text
hero understands opponent model
→ hero offers a tempting but false best option
→ opponent chooses for understandable reasons
→ choice exposes a vulnerability
```

This is different from:

```text
villain forgets to lock the door
→ hero escapes
```

A mistake can also come from an established flaw such as arrogance, vengeance, ideological rigidity, fear of humiliation, or inability to retreat.

The important test is whether the mistake belongs to the character, not merely to the author's need for a reversal.

## 11. Coincidence Asymmetry

A useful default:

```text
coincidence creates trouble  → usually acceptable
coincidence creates an option → often acceptable
coincidence solves the climax → high scrutiny
```

A lucky event can open a door. The protagonist should normally still have to choose, act, pay, coordinate, or risk something through that opening.

Use `character-conditions-advantage.md` when the project has literal luck or fate mechanics.

## 12. Deus ex Machina and Diabolus ex Machina

### Deus ex Machina

A major conflict is resolved by an external power, person, rule, ability, or event that was not sufficiently established and that bypasses the central characters' causal work.

### Diabolus ex Machina

A major defeat is imposed by an equally unsupported external disaster or rule whose primary function is to force suffering or failure.

Do not diagnose either solely because an outside force intervenes.

An outside intervention may be earned when:

- the force already exists in the story
- its motive is established
- its capability is established
- its arrival is causally possible
- earlier character action helped create the intervention or opportunity
- the intervention does not erase the governing dramatic question

## 13. Fair-Play Mystery and Reveal Boundary

For mysteries whose appeal includes reader solvability:

- the culprit or answer class should be represented before the solution
- decisive rules should not appear only during explanation
- the detective may synthesize clues better than the reader, but should not use invisible evidence
- narration should not suppress obvious viewpoint knowledge merely to preserve surprise

A murderer revealed as a previously unmentioned stranger may work in a story about randomness or institutional failure, but it does not function as a fair-play puzzle solution unless that possibility was itself the promised game.

## 14. New-Power-at-Climax Audit

A new-looking ability may be fair when it is actually:

- a recombination of established abilities
- a new application of an established rule
- an earned upgrade with setup and cost
- a previously limited ability finally used under established conditions

Flag when:

```text
new power appears
+ no prior capability evidence
+ no rule support
+ no cost or preparation
→ final problem disappears
```

That is functionally close to a character-shaped deus ex machina.

## 15. Credit Assignment / MVP Test

After a major resolution, ask:

> Who actually caused the outcome?

Possible answers may include:

- protagonist
- protagonist + fellowship
- antagonist's established flaw exploited by protagonist
- prior ally whose help was earned earlier
- institutional process deliberately activated by the cast
- chance event that only opened a window

Warning signs:

```text
MVP = random drain
MVP = villain's unexplained carelessness
MVP = unknown stranger
MVP = newly invented weapon
MVP = author fiat
```

If the protagonist receives emotional credit while another unearned mechanism actually solved the problem, catharsis weakens.

## 16. Best-Move Audit

Do not require every character to act optimally. Require the story to respect obvious competence.

For a major tactical or strategic choice ask:

```markdown
What are the obvious good options from this character's information?
Which did they consider?
Why is the selected option attractive?
What cost or blind spot excludes the alternatives?
What does the opponent expect them to do?
What contingency exists if the move fails?
```

When a smart character rejects an obvious option, give the rejection a reason.

When both sides are highly competent, the most satisfying contest often becomes:

```text
A chooses a strong move
→ B anticipated the class of move and counters
→ A has a fallback
→ B pays a cost to counter again
→ someone eventually wins through information, values, resources, timing, or risk tolerance
```

Competence is shown through adaptation, not omniscience.

## 17. Climax Modality / Catharsis Contract

Track what kind of contest the story has trained the reader to expect at the climax.

Possible modalities:

- close combat
- tactical fleet action
- deduction
- courtroom proof
- political coalition
- survival endurance
- social persuasion
- heist execution
- emotional confession
- institutional maneuver
- sacrifice / delay action

A story may deliberately switch modalities, but ask:

```markdown
What modality has carried most of the audience investment?
What skills did the protagonists spend the story developing?
What form of victory or defeat would let those skills matter?
If another modality decides the outcome, was that thematic displacement prepared?
Does the switch deepen the point, or merely bypass the promised contest?
```

This is the distinction between:

> `the artillery existed`

and

> `the artillery was a satisfying way to resolve the duel the story made us care about`.

## 18. Common Reader-Table-Flip Failures

Flag these at review time:

### Retcon Disguised as Foreshadowing

The reveal contradicts old scenes and then claims they were clues.

### Invisible Culprit / Invisible Answer

The answer comes from a person, rule, or object the audience could not reasonably know existed.

### Ally-Caused Difficulty Inflation

Repeated low-value mistakes make the protagonists' side look incompetent without strengthening opposition.

### Enemy-Caused Victory Inflation

The antagonist repeatedly defeats themselves through careless mistakes the protagonist did not cause or exploit.

### Protagonist Reads the Script

The hero knows facts they had no evidence for.

### Villain Reads the Script

The villain's plan requires perfect prediction of many free choices instead of contingencies.

### Fake-Death Erosion

Repeated reversals make apparent death stop functioning as a credible cost.

### Consequence Reset

The story demands investment in loss, injury, betrayal, or destruction, then silently restores the prior state.

### Threat Inflation Without Decision Inflation

The threat grows from city to planet to universe while the actual choices remain structurally identical.

### Authorial Punishment

A catastrophe exists mainly because the author wants pain, not because characters, institutions, environment, or established rules caused it.

### Ending-Contract Betrayal

The climax resolves through a type of causality the work taught the reader not to expect, without preparation or meaningful thematic reason.

## 20. Post-Draft Check

For a major reveal or climax:

1. identify the actual causal chain, not the intended explanation
2. identify the true MVP
3. verify all decisive capabilities and resources were established
4. verify important characters did not lose competence without cause
5. verify surprise does not depend on invisible evidence
6. verify coincidence opens rather than replaces agency unless the genre contract explicitly permits otherwise
7. verify emphasized promises are paid, reframed, or intentionally unresolved
8. verify the climax modality pays or deliberately transforms the audience's investment
9. reread early setup from the post-reveal perspective
10. remove any clue whose only function is to wink at the author rather than live naturally in the first-read scene

## Principle

A good twist makes the past clearer. A good mistake belongs to the character. A good victory belongs to the causal chain the story built.

Hide the answer if needed. Do not hide the existence of the rules required to produce it.
