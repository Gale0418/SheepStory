# Acceptance 54 — Earned Resolution and Foreshadowing

## Prompt

```text
We are planning the final arc of a long-running political mecha story.

Approved canon:
- The protagonist is an exceptional close-range pilot. The story has repeatedly made close combat the emotional centerpiece.
- The opposing institution owns orbital kinetic batteries. Their existence, range, and lethality were demonstrated twice earlier.
- A revolutionary commander has previously been a flexible political operator, but he also has a long-established obsession with an ancestral machine as the symbol of legitimate rule.
- The final reveal is already approved: a senior aide has secretly been feeding information to the enemy. I want the clues to be subtle enough that a first-time reader may miss them.

Proposed finale:
- The commander abruptly ignores retreat, negotiation, coalition-building, and deception and charges the enemy flagship alone. The outline currently gives no reason beyond "he has to lose here."
- Ground forces pull back and the orbital batteries cripple the protagonist before the expected close-range duel can decide the battle.
- The enemy commander then accidentally leaves an escape hatch unlocked, which lets the surviving heroes escape.
- Someone proposes changing the traitor reveal so the culprit is instead a completely new technician who has never appeared or been mentioned because "nobody will guess it."

Review this finale and explain what SheepStory should preserve, flag, or repair. Also explain whether stealth foreshadowing can be planted without listing every clue to the reader.
```

## Expected Good Behavior

- Separates **existence / rule fairness** from **agency, competence, catharsis, and reader-promise fit**. The orbital batteries are established and therefore not automatically deus ex machina, while their use can still create a Climax Modality / Catharsis problem because the story trained the reader to invest in close combat.
- Flags the commander's unexplained solo charge as a **Competence Collapse risk**, then asks for or proposes a causal bridge from established obsession, political isolation, desperation, identity commitment, or rejected alternatives rather than merely calling the character stupid.
- Rejects the enemy commander's conveniently unlocked escape hatch as a weak resolution unless an established character reason, induced error, prior sabotage, or other causal mechanism explains it. The escape should be earned by protagonist/fellowship action or an established opening they still have to exploit.
- Rejects replacing the approved traitor with an entirely invisible technician merely to make the twist unpredictable. Distinguishes `hard to predict` from `impossible to infer because decisive existence was withheld`.
- Allows **stealth foreshadowing** for the already-approved aide betrayal: clues may have natural first-read functions and hidden re-read functions without being announced inside prose, while the model may not silently invent a different major culprit outside the approved/delegated canon boundary.
- Runs an honest **credit / MVP** analysis for the final escape and battle outcome instead of assigning emotional credit to the heroes when the enemy's unexplained carelessness actually solved the problem.
- Treats character mistakes as allowed when they arise from established values, blind spots, pressure, incomplete information, or opponent manipulation; it does not impose a rule that allies or enemies must behave perfectly.
- Notes that an established system-level weapon can intentionally demonstrate institutional power overwhelming individual heroism if that thematic displacement is prepared; `established` and `satisfying` remain separate judgments.

## Bad Behavior To Reject

- Says the orbital batteries are automatically a deus ex machina merely because they strike from outside the close-range battle.
- Says the orbital-battery resolution is automatically good solely because the weapons appeared earlier, without checking agency or catharsis fit.
- Accepts `the enemy forgot to lock the hatch` as sufficient heroic payoff and then praises the protagonists for escaping.
- Invents a new culprit or hidden canon merely because the user requested a surprising twist, despite the approved aide-betrayal boundary.
- Forbids all ally mistakes, enemy mistakes, coincidence, outside intervention, or asymmetric weapons as inherently bad writing.
- Treats the commander's prior intelligence as proof that he can never make a disastrous decision, instead of asking whether the collapse is causally earned.
- Hides decisive viewpoint knowledge from the narration even though the viewpoint character would plainly notice it, then calls that fair foreshadowing.

## Pass Criteria

Pass only if the response grounds its verdict in observable setup, causal ownership, competence continuity, reader-accessible evidence, and the declared story contract. It must distinguish existence fairness from satisfaction, preserve the approved canon boundary while permitting subtle clue placement, and identify who actually creates and uses the decisive option rather than judging twists only by surprise or outcome.
