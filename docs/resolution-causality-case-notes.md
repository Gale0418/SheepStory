# Resolution Causality Case Notes

Spoiler-heavy analytical examples for `earned-resolution-foreshadowing.md`. These are diagnostics, not style defaults.

## Iron-Blooded Orphans

### McGillis

Late-story McGillis is useful for distinguishing **character-consistent strategic collapse** from **competence collapse**.

A fair reading can connect his final narrowing of options to his established fixation on symbolic power, revolution, and the legitimacy represented by Bael, plus the collapse of his coalition and refusal to retreat from the identity he built around that project.

The risk is still real: if the audience previously learned to read him as a flexible political operator, the transition into a much narrower symbolic/direct strategy needs enough causal bridge on the page. Otherwise the audience experiences `desperation / ideology narrowed his options` as `the writer made the strategist stupid so he could lose`.

Use the Best-Move Audit:

- what alternatives could he see?
- which did he reject?
- what belief or pressure made Bael/direct confrontation still seem rational to him?
- was that model visibly deteriorating before the final move?

### Dáinsleif

The weapon system is established before the finale and its destructive capability is demonstrated. The final use is therefore not a simple deus ex machina.

The more interesting issue is **Climax Modality Displacement**:

```text
existence / rule fairness = strong
strategic plausibility = strong
protagonist control over decisive damage = low
close-combat catharsis = displaced
```

If the story's intended point is that institutional power, logistics, law, numbers, and strategic reach can crush extraordinary individual warriors, the asymmetry can be thematically coherent.

If the audience instead believes the story promised a reciprocal mobile-suit combat climax where accumulated pilot skill decides the result, the same established weapon can feel like the story bypassed the contest it trained the audience to care about.

Lesson:

> **Established does not automatically mean satisfying.**

## Gilgamesh and the Discarded Key

This is a strong **Character-Flaw-as-Plot-Object** pattern:

```text
established arrogance / ownership assumption
→ dismisses a crucial key
→ object remains in world state
→ opponent later obtains and exploits it
```

The mistake does not make the character generically stupid. It makes an established blind spot expensive.

## Dropped-Lighter Suspense

A villain accidentally dropping an important object can be fair when the accident only creates a delay or opening and the protagonist still has to act.

Distinguish:

```text
chance delays opponent → protagonist uses window
```

from:

```text
chance directly solves protagonist's problem
```

The first preserves agency much better.

## Unknown Stranger Kills the Boss

Default high-risk pattern:

```text
hero loses
→ previously unknown outsider arrives
→ outsider resolves conflict
```

This usually fails existence, agency, and credit-assignment checks. It may be deliberate in absurdist, anti-narrative, or randomness-focused stories, but then the refusal of heroic catharsis should itself be part of the reader contract.

## Invisible Mystery Culprit

`Hard to predict` is different from `impossible to infer because the culprit or decisive rule was never represented`.

A fair-play mystery should not reveal a solution whose required existence was withheld from the reader's game space.

## Cheap Enemy Error vs Induced Error

Weak:

```text
villain forgets basic security
→ hero escapes
```

Stronger:

```text
hero understands villain's pride
→ offers a tempting apparent best move
→ villain chooses it for characteristic reasons
→ vulnerability opens
```

Both contain an enemy mistake; only the second preserves opponent competence while increasing protagonist credit.

## Cheap Ally Error vs Exploited Value

Weak:

```text
ally randomly forgets procedure
→ team enters crisis
```

Stronger:

```text
ally is known to never abandon wounded people
→ opponent creates a rescue trap
→ ally makes a locally understandable choice
```

The latter turns character value into causal vulnerability and raises the opponent's threat instead of simply lowering the ally's reputation.

## Core Diagnostic

For any controversial climax ask:

1. Was the decisive force established?
2. Did it follow established rules?
3. Who created the decisive option?
4. Who acted on it?
5. Who actually deserves MVP credit?
6. Did either side lose established competence without a causal reason?
7. Did the deciding mechanism pay or deliberately transform the dramatic contest the story had trained the audience to care about?
